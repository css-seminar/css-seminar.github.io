## All speakers

- The audience is CS & Stats PhD Students primarily but other students and faculty may attend. 
- Our students want to hear a bit about your career path too. Whether you start or end with it they really love seeing the human side of a career and how that relates to the technical work. 
- Students write really positive comments about talks that have a few slides at the start with diagrams or heavily annotated equations that convey the key ideas and give an overview of where things are going. Even a toy example at the start can help a lot and the work will be more theoretical, a concrete illustration at the start helps students follow along. 



## Industry Speakers

- Be careful with product names: students will generally know the underlying technolgy of a lot more products than they know the specific commercial product names of, especially brand new or products that do not have free academic access. It helps a lot to say the *technology type/category* along with a product name.  For example, saying, "our LLM provider, AWS bedrock" instead of just "AWS bedrock." This is especially true for products that are used in work, that we wouldn't use in teaching like productivity apps/ticketing systems etc. 
- It's an international audience: PhD students quite often earn their undergraduate degree abroad and come to the US only for graduate studies. Take care with turns of phrase/idoms that might be US-specific. 



## Academic Speakers


- The students will have an assignment to look up jargon from the abstract, so including key jargon in the abstract will help them prepare. 
- The PhD students each have to take 1 course in the other area, but most take a course that is close to their own work in the other area.  For example, the Stats students typically be familiar with a lot of computer architecture or programming langauge theory, they'll have a strong math background to be able to grasp, but need a couple extra steps rather than jargon. In contrast, the CS students mostly are not familiar with causal inference, but they get the general data processing/ predictive ML ideas. 
- The students to not all necessarily have a good "map" of subdisciplines and how they fit together. Giving some idea of what your subarea is and the main organizing questions/goals can help students both understand the talk and is one of the key outcomes we hope students get from attending a broad seminar.
